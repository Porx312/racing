The pedal splits braking by bias. **Engine braking** adds another layer: it acts only on the driven wheels, like light extra braking. You are not in neutral—that effect is always there.

## The idea

Label bias (55/45, etc.) is not real bias when you downshift. In **RWD**, engine braking loads the rear and can unsettle it. In **FWD**, it loads the axle that also steers. In **AWD** the effect is more neutral.

Controlling **when** you downshift is part of braking and entry balance.

## Why it works

On a downshift the driven axle sees negative torque. Stab a short gear too early and you change balance before trail starts. A clean blip and a later downshift keep the brake peak predictable.

This matters on a straight and even more while turning: the same “light brake” on the driven axle rotates or pushes depending on layout.

## On track

A 3rd-to-2nd corner in RWD. Stab 2nd too early and the rear gets nervous before trail. Delay the shift with a clean blip and the peak feels more stable.

In FWD, the same early downshift can saturate the nose under braking or push on entry.

## Typical mistakes

- Ignoring the driven axle
- Inconsistent blip (shocks)
- Early downshift “from street habit”
- Blaming brake bias when the problem is shift timing

Same corner and brake mark.

**5** entries with an early downshift and **5** with the downshift just before turn-in (consistent blip).

Note which one pushes or rotates too much. Keep the more stable option at **95%** pace.
