On flat ground the threshold is a line. With elevation it **dances**. Braking is an active input: read load and adjust.

## The idea

- **Compression:** the road pushes the car → more grip → you can brake harder.
- **Crest:** the road falls away → less grip → the threshold drops.
- **Bumps / patches:** the same at small scale; a fixed peak over a bump often locks.

The same zone can climb, crest, and drop: the brake trace stops being a clean triangle.

## Why it works

Without vertical load the tire cannot turn pedal pressure into clean deceleration. Hold the peak over a crest and you saturate. Miss a compression and you leave grip on the table.

On high DF the effect stacks with speed-based release: more complexity, same rule—**follow the load**.

## On track

A blind braking zone that climbs, crosses a crest, then drops (think Corkscrew). On the crest the wheel goes light—you ease. When support returns you can rebuild pressure before turn-in.

Watch color changes or cracks in the asphalt: they often mark bumps that punish the peak.

## Typical mistakes

- Treating the whole zone as constant grip
- Locking on the bump by not modulating
- Easing too much on the crest and never rebuilding
- Blaming brake bias when the problem is elevation

<Exercise>
Find a crest or bump in your braking zone.

**10** reps: cut 10 to 20 percent across it, then rebuild.

Compare lockups with **5** “flat” reps. Goal: fewer lockups with the conscious adjustment.
</Exercise>
