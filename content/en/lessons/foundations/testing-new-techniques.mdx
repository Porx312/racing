Testing a technique is not “today I drive differently”. It is an experiment: measure a baseline, apply **one** change, compare, and decide whether it stays.

## The idea

Without a baseline you do not know whether grip, traffic, or luck explain the result. The protocol isolates the variable—terminal trail pressure or turn-in timing, for example—from the rest of your driving.

The order is always the same: **baseline → change → return to the old style → decide on averages**, not on a single purple lap.

## Why it works

Grip and traffic add noise. One purple lap proves nothing. Returning to the previous style after the change block confirms whether the gain was real or just adrenaline.

It also protects your reference: if you change and forget how you braked before, you can no longer compare. The protocol keeps the control.

## On track

You want to release the brake later in a medium-speed corner. If you start releasing late with no baseline, you will not know whether new understeer comes from trail or from carrying 3 km/h more entry speed.

First measure five laps in your current style (same mark, same entry target). Then eight with one change only. Then three back to the old style. Look at sector average, not the highlight.

## Typical mistakes

- Judging on a single clean lap
- Changing and forgetting the previous style
- Moving the brake mark and the release at the same time
- Declaring victory because it “feels better” with no numbers logged

<Exercise>
Pick one corner and one metric (sector time or minimum speed).

1. **5** baseline laps in your current style.
2. **8** laps with one change only.
3. **3** laps back to the old style.

Keep the change only if it wins on **average**, not on the best single lap. Log all three averages.
</Exercise>
