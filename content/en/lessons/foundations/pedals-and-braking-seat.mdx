Under hard braking the seat must hold you so the foot only thinks about pressure. If you brace on the pedal, metering stops being clean.

## The idea

The “braking seat” is the anchor: lumbar and back take the load so heel and foot can build a repeatable peak. A sliding body adds a second shove on the pedal—the famous double pump—and the tire sees two peaks instead of one.

Pedal distance, height, and deck angle matter as much as the wheel. If the heel floats, you will never have a stable threshold.

## Why it works

Weight transfer to the front axle is predictable only if the brake input is predictable. A clean ramp-up loads the nose; a late shove saturates when you were already fine.

On a load cell, the temptation is to “rest” the body on the pedal. That turns the brake into a human anti-roll bar. Result: inconsistent peaks and lockups on the second shove.

## On track

On a high-speed straight your heel floats. You hit 80%, reshape, then shove to 95%. ABS (or lockup) appears on the second peak, not the first. It feels like a grip problem; it is an anchor problem.

Fix seat and distance before blaming tires. Then train one continuous ramp to a chosen percentage.

## Typical mistakes

- Heel in the air
- Using the brake to hold yourself in the seat
- Pedals too far “because the real car…”
- Hunting threshold by moving the mark instead of cleaning the peak

<Exercise>
**10 stops** on a straight to ~40 km/h.

Goal: one continuous ramp to a chosen % (e.g. 90) **with no double pump**.

Count how many of 10 are clean. If you miss more than 3, move the seat closer or raise lumbar support before continuing.
</Exercise>
